I’m Cameron MacQuarrie, a cell biologist at Arcadia Science. Human biology gets modelled in the same few species out of habit rather than fit. I work on choosing better ones — then getting the imaging good enough to tell whether the choice was right.

01 — Work

What I actually do

Finding the right organism for the question

Evolution has already run the experiment. Across the eukaryotic tree there are organisms carrying proteins far more human-like than their phylogenetic distance would predict — and those are the ones worth modelling a disease in. We score that with a statistical distance over the physicochemical properties of predicted protein structures, which is what sits behind Zoogle.

  • Organism selection
  • Protein structure
  • Disease models

Then actually looking at it

A prediction is only worth as much as the assay behind it. Picking an unusual organism means there is rarely an established way to see anything in it, so most of my time goes into quantitative live-cell imaging, high-content and 3D phenotyping, and working out what a phenotype even looks like in a species nobody has measured.

  • Live imaging
  • Phenotyping
  • Microscopy

Earlier: the cytoskeleton, up close

My PhD and postdoc were spent on branched actin — how a cell positions the machinery that builds it. In fission yeast, the adaptor Bbc1 turns out to control where the WASP protein Wsp1 sits inside an endocytic patch, making position itself a form of regulation. The endocytosis sandbox below is built from that thesis.
